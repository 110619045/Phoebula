2. Q & A

  • What was it inspired by?

    Our group members come from China, Taiwan, and South Korea. Most of us understand Mandarin, but our Korean teammate does not. To ensure that everyone could participate equally, he suggested that we avoid speaking Chinese during group discussions.

    This experience made us realize how strongly language can affect participation, inclusion, and power within a group. It also showed us that language restrictions could become an effective design medium. We therefore began developing our game around the question: “Who decides when you are allowed to speak, and in which language?”


  • What was the format?

    Hell's Restaurant is a card-based social negotiation game. Each player draws a communication-restriction card that determines how they are allowed to communicate, such as English only, Chinese only, Korean only, gestures only, adjectives only, or a maximum of three words at a time.

    Each player also receives a hidden personal preference. While attempting to fulfill this individual goal, the group must complete a shared task: agreeing on a three-course meal consisting of one appetizer, one main course, and one dessert within the given time.

    The game uses a menu, communication-restriction cards, hidden preference cards, and group shared goal cards. The restrictions are visible to everyone, while each player's preference remains private. This creates a conflict between the players' individual goals, the group's shared goal, and their unequal ability to communicate.


  • How Does This Design Respond to the Prompt?

    In Repairing Play, the reading points out that traditional theories of play have often excluded Black voices, language, and lived experiences. This made us consider how the subject–object relationship is closely connected to voice: Who is allowed to speak? Who defines the rules? Whose experience is understood as legitimate?

    As international students, we have also experienced moments when speaking in a second language made us feel positioned more like objects than active subjects. When we cannot clearly express our thoughts or emotions, other people may interpret our meaning for us, ignore our ideas, or control the direction of the conversation.

    This feeling became especially visible in our group because our Korean teammate was the only person who could not understand Mandarin. When the people around someone begin speaking a language that person cannot understand, that person can quickly feel excluded from the conversation.

    Our game turns this experience into a playable structure. Although everyone is working toward the same task, each player must participate under a different communication restriction. Some players can speak freely, while others must depend on gestures, limited vocabulary, or a language they may not know. As a result, the players do not have equal power to express themselves, influence the group, or have their preferences included in the final decision.

    The subject–object positions are not completely fixed. A player may become an object when their ability to communicate is restricted, but they may also become a subject when they enforce another player’s restriction, interpret someone else’s gestures, or take control of the discussion. Through these shifting roles, the game explores how language can grant, limit, and redistribute power within a group.

  • Any surprises or reflections on the actual experience? Were there interactions that you didn’t expect? Did you over-explain an instruction?

    The overall atmosphere of the game was close to what we had expected. The helplessness experienced by players with language restrictions highlighted the freedom and conversational power of players without those restrictions.

    To make the game more challenging and entertaining, we had added rules preventing players from pointing directly at the menu or saying the names of dishes. Although this group did not consistently follow these rules, they still found it extremely difficult to communicate and reach an agreement. We were surprised by how powerful the language restrictions were on their own. Even fifteen minutes was not enough for seven players to complete the task. They reached the dessert discussion but did not finalize their choices or place an order.

    Therefore, rather than over-explaining the instructions, we may have overcomplicated the game by adding unnecessary rules. The communication restrictions already created enough difficulty and tension without the additional restrictions.

    One situation we especially wanted to observe was what would happen if someone who could not speak Chinese or Korean drew a Chinese Only or Korean Only card. Formally, that player still had the same right to speak as everyone else. In practice, however, the mismatch between the restriction and the player’s language ability took away their ability to participate. They had permission to speak, but lacked an accessible way to exercise that permission.

    Another unexpected interaction involved the player who drew the Free2 card. Although this player theoretically had more freedom and power than the others, he did not fully understand the rules and also experienced a language barrier when communicating with the group. As a result, they became disconnected from the discussion. Although the game had assigned him the position of a subject, the actual interaction pushed him into the position of an object.

    This showed us that the subject–object relationship cannot be determined by a role card alone. A person may be formally given freedom and authority but still lose agency because of their language ability, understanding of the situation, or relationship with the group. Subject and object positions are therefore not fixed; they are continuously shaped by the social interaction among the players.


  • Personal Curiosities and Reflections

    While facilitating the game, I realized that we, as the designers and facilitators, were also placed in the position of subjects, while our classmates became objects who had to listen to our instructions and follow the rules we created.

    During the game, I found that being a subject was not necessarily easier than being an object. The subject had the authority to control the situation and could have chosen not to care about the other players’ needs. Instead, the subject made the greatest effort to coordinate the group and help everyone participate.

    During the teacher-led discussion after the game, the subject from another group shared a similar experience. They felt responsible for the group’s success. Even when some players’ expressions were difficult to understand, the subject still had to make sure that everyone’s voice was heard. At the same time, they had to decide which voices and needs should be prioritized.

    This player was trying to manage three different goals:
    1. The entire meal had to cost less than $40.
    2. His hidden personal preference was to choose traditional dishes.
    3. He also wanted to listen to and consider the other players’ needs.

    The player described the strangest part of the experience as having to prioritize certain things over others: “No matter what, I had to place some things above others. Even though the stakes of the game were low, I still questioned myself: Why did I make that choice? Was it really the right thing to do?”

    While observing my group, I could see that the subject was genuinely trying hard to coordinate everyone. She often looked confused as she attempted to understand the restricted players. Ironically, she was also the person who laughed the loudest at their attempts to communicate haha.

    This contradiction was especially interesting to me. A subject can sincerely try to listen to and support others while still unintentionally treating them as objects of observation or amusement. Meanwhile, the objects were not completely passive. Their frustration often made them more eager to seize every available opportunity to express their opinions. This experience showed me that subject and object are not simple or fixed roles. Their positions are continuously negotiated through responsibility, interpretation, attention, and interaction.
