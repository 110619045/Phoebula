What Is Interactivity to Me?

To begin with, I think it's important to define what interactivity actually means. Interactive means involving or encouraging a two-way flow of action, communication, or information between a user and a system, device, or person.

To me, interactivity is a bridge that connects people with different media, allowing them to build relationships. There are many forms of interaction. Through our different senses, we can design various ways for people to interact with almost anything. And these interactions can create different kinds of relationships.

For example, a simple button on a website can lead to completely different experiences. If clicking the button opens a spreadsheet, the interaction is mainly functional. It helps the user complete a task. But what if clicking the same button opens a surprise box? Suddenly, the interaction is no longer just about functionality. It might create excitement, curiosity, or even disappointment. Although the physical action is exactly the same, the experience and emotional response can be completely different.

This is why I believe the most important part of designing interactions is not the interaction itself, but what we want people to experience through it. Interactivity doesn't necessarily make an artwork or a story better. It depends on whether the interaction serves a meaningful purpose. Sometimes, interaction can deepen emotional engagement or create a stronger connection with the work. Other times, it might simply become a distraction.

Ultimately, I think good interaction design is about understanding what kind of relationship we want to create between people and the work, and choosing the right form of interaction to make that relationship possible.

Is Interactivity Necessary in Art and Storytelling?

But is interactivity really necessary in every form of art?

I think it depends on what kind of experience the artist wants to create. If an artwork tells a complete, predetermined story, too much audience interaction might interrupt its rhythm. However, if the story is meant to be explored, discovered, or pieced together by the audience, interactivity becomes much more important.

Take movies, for example. Film is a form of artistic expression, and I think most people would agree that a good movie can be deeply immersive even when we are simply sitting and watching. Movies usually have a carefully structured storyline, and as the story unfolds, we gradually become immersed in its world.

If we were suddenly asked to perform an action in the middle of a movie, it might actually pull our attention back to the physical world and interrupt that sense of immersion.

However, there are some interesting exceptions. In Taiwan, concert films are quite popular, and theaters sometimes organize special screenings where audiences are encouraged to cheer, sing along, and respond together, just as they would at a live concert.

In this case, interaction doesn't interrupt the experience. Instead, it becomes part of the experience itself. The goal is not necessarily to discover a new story, but to recreate the feeling of being at a live concert and sharing that excitement with others.

This makes me think that the value of interactivity in art depends on how we want the audience to experience the work.

Is watching and listening enough to create immersion? What about our other senses? What would happen if we introduced temperature, humidity, vibration, or other physical sensations? Would these elements deepen the audience's connection to the work, or would they simply distract them?

Ultimately, I don't think interactivity is something that should be added to an artwork just to make it more engaging. Instead, we should ask whether it helps communicate the intended experience, and whether the interaction itself contributes something meaningful to the story.
